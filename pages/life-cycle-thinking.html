Pillar 03 — Life-Cycle Thinking

Every product has a before and an after. Trace both.

Life-cycle thinking means asking: where did the materials come from, what happens during use, and where does it go after we are done? Formalized as Life Cycle Assessment (LCA, ISO 14040/14044), it is how credible eco-labels verify their claims.

Concept

Cradle-to-grave — and beyond to cradle-to-cradle

Cradle-to-grave follows a product from raw extraction (“cradle”) through disposal (“grave”). It reveals burden-shifting — e.g., a “recyclable” pack that is never collected, or an energy-efficient device made with conflict minerals.

Cradle-to-cradle (McDonough & Braungart, 2002) redesigns the grave: either technical nutrients circulate in closed industrial loops, or biological nutrients safely return to the biosphere. Waste equals food. The Cradle to Cradle Certified label audits exactly that.

Linear (cradle-to-grave)

Take → Make → Use → Dispose

Goal: reduce harm within a one-way flow.

Circular (cradle-to-cradle)

Borrow → Make → Use → Return → Remake

Goal: eliminate the concept of waste.

What the numbers mean for choices

  • Regenerative or recycled cotton addresses farming + manufacturing — the two biggest hotspots. GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard) certifies both fiber and processing.
  • Dark dyes omitted, undyed or naturally dyed options lower chemical manufacturing impact — EU Ecolabel audits this via REACH compliance.
  • Wash cold, wash less, repair shrinks the use phase — the only stage fully in consumer control.
  • Second-hand or take-back (e.g., Patagonia Worn Wear, H&M Garment Collecting with caution) defers landfill and avoids new production.

Design lens — beyond the consumer

LCA is also a design tool: Fairphone maps minerals to avoid cobalt hotspots; IKEA’s Product Environmental Footprint pilot scores sofas; Cradle to Cradle Certified requires material health inventories. The consumer reads the label; the designer runs the assessment.

Second traced example — smartphone (brief)

Mining (rare earths, cobalt) dominates social/ecological impact; assembly energy ~30% of CO₂; use-phase electricity (~3 years) another ~30%; e-waste recovery via EPEAT/WEEE take-back is critical — only ~17% of global e-waste is formally recycled (UN Global E-waste Monitor 2024).
